The Cafeteria That Stopped Using Trays

Last September, the main cafeteria at Hsin-Ming University quietly removed its trays. Students who were used to loading up with several plates suddenly had to carry what they could in two hands. At first, many complained on social media, calling the change annoying and a cost-cutting trick.

Six months later, the numbers tell a different story. Manager Wang Li-hua says that the food thrown away per student has fallen by 24 percent, and the cafeteria uses about 15 percent less water because no trays need washing. "When you can't pile everything up, you take only what you really want, and you come back if you're still hungry," Wang explains.

Not everyone benefits equally. Students with disabilities and those carrying heavy bags found the new system difficult. After a meeting with the Student Union, the cafeteria agreed to keep a small stack of trays at the counter, available to anyone who asks.

Encouraged by the results, the university now plans to go further. From next semester, the cafeteria will offer half-size portions at a lower price, and leftover cooked vegetables will be turned into compost for campus gardens instead of being sent to a landfill. A student survey in June will show whether people are happy with the changes.

What Losing a Startup Competition Taught Me

When my friends and I entered the National Student Startup Challenge last spring, we were sure we would win. Our idea was an app called SeatFinder, which shows which library seats are free in real time. We had spent two months building it, and everyone we talked to on campus said they would use it.

We made it to the final round, but the judges' questions destroyed us. "How will you make money?" one asked. "Who pays for the sensors?" asked another. We had focused so much on the technology that we had never prepared a budget. We finished ninth out of ten teams.

I was embarrassed for weeks. Then Ms. Kuo, a mentor at the university's Innovation Center, told me something I have not forgotten: "The judges didn't reject your idea. They rejected your homework." She encouraged us to talk to the people who would actually pay: library managers.

Those conversations changed everything. We learned that libraries cared less about helping students find seats than about knowing which areas were used most. We rebuilt SeatFinder as a tool that gives libraries usage reports, and this fall two university libraries are testing it. We still don't know whether it will succeed as a business, but I now ask "Who will pay?" before "What can we build?"
